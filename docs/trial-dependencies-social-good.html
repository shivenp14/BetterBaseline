When information changes during a clinical trial, a dependency graph can show which documents, decisions, and participant support plans need review.

The proposed addition connects study requirements to participant needs and community support commitments. It helps teams identify who faces new participation barriers and follow through on the support they need.

How this ties to the Social Good track

The community benefit is preventing trial changes from making participation harder for people who already face barriers. Additional travel, missed work, childcare, caregiver needs, or accessibility requirements can place unequal burdens on participants.

For example, a trial adds a visit and its documents are updated, but transport assistance still covers only the original visits. A participant who can drive may manage the change; someone relying on funded transport may have to pay, miss work, or miss the visit. The system exposes that gap and assigns it to someone responsible for resolving it.

  • Fairer access: people with limited income, caregiving responsibilities, or disabilities can receive the support needed to participate.
  • Less unexpected burden: participants can understand additional time and cost before deciding whether to continue.
  • Accountability: promised support is tracked through changes, with unresolved gaps visible to coordinators and authorized community partners.

A graph that only updates clinical documents has an indirect Social Good connection. Participant needs and support commitments must be central to the product to make this connection convincing.

What the demo must establish: a specific barrier is identified, a responsible owner reviews it, and a support commitment is confirmed. An alert alone does not demonstrate that the barrier has been resolved.

How a working implementation would behave

Use a versioned dependency graph with explicit rules that check whether participant support still satisfies the study’s requirements. The graph finds potentially affected records; the rules determine which gaps need review.

  1. Extract the original plan. AI reads protocol and support documents, proposing structured facts and relationships with paragraph citations. A reviewer confirms them.
  2. Compare versions. When an amendment arrives, identify changed visit dates, durations, locations, procedures, or attendance requirements. Proposed changes remain distinct from approved requirements.
  3. Follow affected relationships. Traverse from changed requirements to dependent document claims, participant needs, and support commitments.
  4. Recalculate constraints. Check whether coverage, availability, and capacity still satisfy the new requirements.
  5. Create review tasks. Include the triggering change, dependency path, source evidence, and accountable owner.
  6. Record resolution. Close a finding after an authorized reviewer confirms a document revision or support commitment. Preserve earlier versions and decisions.

AI extracts and explains; explicit rules calculate the gaps. AI can draft wording or suggest options. A human or verified service record must establish that funding, a booking, or provider availability has actually been confirmed.

What the graph stores

  • Study requirements: visit date, duration, location, procedures, and whether attendance must be in person.
  • Participant needs: voluntarily declared transport, childcare, caregiver, or accessibility needs. Use fictional profiles for the prototype.
  • Support commitments: trips covered, reimbursement limits, service availability, capacity, and expiration dates.
  • Document claims: specific statements such as “four visits are required,” linked to their paragraph and source version.
  • Review decisions: who confirmed a relationship, approved a revision, or verified a support commitment, and when.

Relationships carry meaning: a visit requires attendance; a participant needs transport for that attendance; an agreement covers certain trips; a paragraph describes the schedule. Each relationship stores its source, source version, confidence, and confirmation status.

required trips > covered trips
    → transport coverage gap

new visit day outside provider availability
    → transport availability needs review

current document visit count ≠ approved protocol visit count
    → document revision required
A changed source does not automatically make an older document wrong. A historical report may correctly describe a frozen dataset or prior protocol. Distinguish “used this version,” “calculated from this value,” “assumes this remains true,” and “must reflect the current value.”

A second example: a support agreement expires

A community transport agreement expires while the protocol remains unchanged. The system follows the agreement’s relationships to future visits and participants who depend on it. It creates tasks to confirm replacement coverage and review participant materials that promise transport support.

This shows that a change can originate in a community resource as well as a trial document. The useful finding is the concrete coverage gap, not a prediction that someone will drop out.

A focused first prototype

Use one fictional trial, two protocol versions, three related documents, ten fictional participant profiles, and a few support agreements. Store records in ordinary nodes, edges, versions, and findings tables; a specialized graph database is optional.

Demo sequence: import an amendment → inspect affected dependencies → see evidence-backed gaps → assign review tasks → record confirmed resolutions.

Compare the consequences across declared needs. The same additional visit may create uncovered travel for one participant, childcare expenses for another, and unavailable accessible transport for another. Concrete differences make equitable access visible without inventing dropout probabilities.
