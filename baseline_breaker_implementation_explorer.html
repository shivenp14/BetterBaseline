Where the technical depth lives

The project is a statistical engine with transparent math—not a generative chatbot giving untraceable clinical advice.

Model A

Trial outcomes

XC ~ Binomial(nC, pC)
XT ~ Binomial(nT, pT)

Individual randomized participants with binary endpoints and independent arms. Conditional statistical power depends on the assumed effect and data-generating model.

Model B

Effect assumptions

RR fixed: pT = RR × pC
ARR fixed: pT = pC − Δ

A baseline shock changes the expected number of events. A fixed relative effect does not imply a fixed absolute effect.

Model C

Sample-size pressure

n ≈ [zα√(2p̄(1−p̄)) + zβ√(pC(1−pC)+pT(1−pT))]² / (pC−pT)²

Smaller absolute risk differences lead to sharply higher required enrollment; calculate and round arm sizes explicitly.

Analytical workflow

Deterministic calculation

  1. Validate pC, effect definition, α, power, allocation, and dropout.
  2. Resolve pT from RR or absolute reduction without silently clipping.
  3. Calculate conditional power for candidate analyzable arm sizes.
  4. Double the upper bound, then binary-search for the smallest passing integer N.
  5. Inflate planned enrollment for dropout, and report method + assumptions.
Stochastic workflow

Vectorized simulation

  1. Use numpy.random.default_rng(seed) for a reproducible stream.
  2. Generate arm-level retention/outcome samples across many hypothetical trials.
  3. Apply the predeclared pooled two-proportion z test (or exact branch).
  4. Fraction significant estimates conditional power; show MC SE.
  5. Sweep baseline × dropout or effect size; cache identical requests.
Analytic ≠ probabilistic success prediction

Conditional power

Probability of a significant result under a specific assumed treatment effect and model. This does not assert the drug has that effect.

Uncertainty integrated power

Optional weighted average over declared baseline/effect distributions. It is only meaningful if the distributions and their dependencies are justified.

Trial success probability

Requires beliefs about true efficacy, design success criteria, and other uncertainty—not automatically given by the first two.

07 / Scientific quality

Prove the answers deserve trust.

A health-research statistics tool lives or dies by its assumptions, numerical correctness, and reproducibility.

Validation ladder

2 · Reference comparisonstatsmodels on a scenario matrix

4 · End-to-endReal and synthetic preset; offline fallback; deploy smoke test

Known statistical boundaries

  • Near-zero or near-one probabilities can break normal approximations.
  • Low expected cell counts require warnings and/or a Fisher-exact path.
  • Independent dropout is not realistic for every missing-data mechanism.
  • Two-sided, single primary comparison only; no interim looks or multiplicity.
  • No observed post hoc power; do not reconstruct design quality from significance alone.
  • Estimands, endpoint ascertainment and protocol deviations affect interpretation.
Reference implementation comparison: use statsmodels.stats.proportion.proportion_effectsize and statsmodels.stats.power.NormalIndPower for an independently defined benchmark. The dashboard's preview uses a pooled-null two-proportion normal approximation, so results can differ slightly from arcsine-effect-size formulations; document method and tolerance rather than promising identity.

Baseline Breaker: Technical Blueprint for a Clinical-Trial Assumption Stress Tester

Executive summary

Baseline Breaker should not try to design a clinical trial for researchers. It should answer a narrower, technically defensible question:

“If the assumptions used to plan this trial are wrong, how badly does the trial’s statistical performance change?”

That is a substantially stronger hackathon concept than an AI trial-design assistant. The core product is a statistical simulation engine: a researcher enters the assumptions underlying a proposed two-arm randomized trial—control-group event rate, expected treatment effect, dropout rate, significance threshold, desired power, and enrollment—and Baseline Breaker calculates the planned design, then stress-tests it across thousands of plausible alternative realities.

This is well aligned with actual clinical-trial methodology. FDA's adoption of ICH E9(R1) explicitly emphasizes defining the treatment effect of interest and using sensitivity analysis to understand how assumptions and data limitations affect interpretation. [research citation in original report]

The crucial insight is that a trial can look perfectly powered under its planning assumptions while becoming substantially underpowered if the actual control-group event rate is different. For example, under a simple two-group normal approximation, detecting a reduction from 20% to 15% at 80% power and a two-sided 5% significance level requires roughly 906 participants per arm. If the actual baseline rate were 10% and the same 25% relative risk reduction held, that enrollment would provide only about 47% asymptotic power under the same approximation. That is exactly the sort of cause-and-effect result your demo should make visually obvious. The implementation can be independently checked against statsmodels' two-sample power implementation. [research citation in original report]

The recommended hackathon scope

MVP: individually randomized, two-arm, superiority trial with a binary primary endpoint.

Build these five things extremely well:

  1. Analytical sample-size and power calculator.
  2. Vectorized Monte Carlo trial simulator.
  3. Baseline-event-rate × dropout sensitivity heatmap.
  4. Interactive "assumption shock" controls that update power curves.
  5. One curated real-trial example plus one guaranteed synthetic demo.

Everything else—cluster randomization, stratification, survival analysis, probabilistic historical priors, automated protocol parsing—should be treated as an extension.

That scope is achievable by four strong CS students in 24–36 hours and still gives you enough statistics, simulation, backend engineering, data ingestion, visualization, reproducibility, and testing to produce a serious technical project rather than a thin LLM wrapper.

Product definition and assumptions

What exactly is a "baseline"?

This distinction is critical, especially given your earlier concern about TrialAtlas.

For Baseline Breaker, the baseline is not:

“What fraction of people in the United States have this disease?”

It is:

“Among patients who satisfy this trial's eligibility criteria and receive the control treatment, what proportion are expected to experience the primary endpoint during the specified follow-up period?”

Those are completely different quantities.

Suppose a heart-failure trial has:

  • patients aged 40–80,
  • specific ejection-fraction requirements,
  • previous hospitalization criteria,
  • certain medications required,
  • kidney-function exclusions,
  • and a primary outcome of hospitalization or cardiovascular death within 12 months.

The baseline relevant to the power calculation is something like:

[ P(\text{primary endpoint by 12 months} \mid \text{eligible trial population, control arm}) ]

It is not simply:

[ P(\text{heart failure in general population}) ]

This is why CDC PLACES should be an optional contextual source, not your source of truth. PLACES publishes modeled small-area health estimates using sources including BRFSS, Census population estimates, and ACS data across counties, census tracts, places, and ZIP Code Tabulation Areas. [research citation in original report] Those data describe populations, not the protocol-defined control-arm outcome risk of an eligible trial population. Therefore, using PLACES directly as the expected control event rate would generally be an invalid leap; that is an inference from the difference between what PLACES measures and what a trial power model needs. [research citation in original report]

The actual problem Baseline Breaker solves

Imagine the statisticians planning a hypothetical trial believe:

[ p_C = 0.20 ]

where (p_C) is the control-arm event probability.

They expect the treatment to reduce risk by 25%, so:

[ RR = 0.75 ]

and therefore:

[ p_T = 0.20(0.75)=0.15. ]

The sample-size calculation is built around the difference between 20% and 15%.

But what happens if the real control event probability is only 10%?

Under the same relative effect:

[ p_T = 0.10(0.75)=0.075. ]

There are now substantially fewer events, and the absolute difference has shrunk from five percentage points to 2.5 percentage points.

The same enrollment can therefore have much less statistical power.

That becomes your product's central interaction:

Planned reality: 20% baseline → 80% planned power
Observed/plausible reality: 10% baseline → ~47% power
Baseline shock: −50%
Consequence: high risk of an inconclusive trial under those assumptions

The user then drags the baseline slider and watches the entire statistical picture change.

○ Constant absolute risk difference

This is important because these produce completely different stress tests.

If relative risk stays constant:

Explicit project assumptions

For the hackathon, state these assumptions directly in the product:

Baseline Breaker MVP assumptions

The primary endpoint is binary; participants are individually randomized between two independent arms; the trial is a superiority design; the primary hypothesis test is two-sided; the MVP uses large-sample two-proportion approximations unless Fisher's exact test is explicitly selected; dropout is initially modeled as independent of outcome; treatment effects are expressed as either relative risk or absolute risk difference; simulations do not replace a protocol statistician or regulatory sample-size analysis.

Statistical engine

This is the core of the project and where you can make Baseline Breaker look much more sophisticated than a typical hackathon application.

Analytical power model

For the MVP, consider two independent arms:

[ X_C \sim \text{Binomial}(n_C,p_C) ]

[ X_T \sim \text{Binomial}(n_T,p_T) ]

where:

  • (C) = control,
  • (T) = treatment,
  • (p_C) = control event probability,
  • (p_T) = treatment event probability.

For equal allocation, a common large-sample approximation to required sample size per arm is:

[ n \approx \frac{ \left[ z_{1-\alpha/2} \sqrt{2\bar p(1-\bar p)} + z_{1-\beta} \sqrt{p_C(1-p_C)+p_T(1-p_T)} \right]^2 }{ (p_T-p_C)^2 } ]

where:

[ \bar p=\frac{p_C+p_T}{2}. ]

The crucial denominator is:

[ (p_T-p_C)^2. ]

When the absolute separation between the arms gets smaller, required enrollment grows rapidly.

For production calculations in the hackathon, I would implement the calculation through the standardized two-proportion effect size as a reference pathway:

[ h = 2\arcsin(\sqrt{p_T})

2\arcsin(\sqrt{p_C}) ]

Statsmodels exposes this quantity through proportion_effectsize, and its independent normal-power solver can solve for sample size, power, significance level, effect size, or allocation ratio. [research citation in original report]

But also implement your own integer sample-size solver, then validate it against statsmodels.

That gives you a much better technical story:

“We didn't just call a library. We implemented the search algorithm ourselves and use statsmodels as an independent reference implementation.”

    while analytic_power(
        n_control=high,
        n_treatment=ceil(high * allocation_ratio),
        p_control=p_control,
        p_treatment=p_treatment,
        alpha=alpha,
    ) < target_power:
        high *= 2

    # Integer binary search.
    while low < high:
        mid = (low + high) // 2

        power = analytic_power(
            n_control=mid,
            n_treatment=ceil(mid * allocation_ratio),
            p_control=p_control,
            p_treatment=p_treatment,
            alpha=alpha,
        )

        if power >= target_power:
            high = mid
        else:
            low = mid + 1

    evaluable_control = low
    evaluable_treatment = ceil(low * allocation_ratio)

    # Enrollment inflation for independent dropout.
    enroll_control = ceil(
        evaluable_control / (1.0 - dropout_control)
    )

    enroll_treatment = ceil(
        evaluable_treatment / (1.0 - dropout_treatment)
    )

    return SampleSizeResult(...)

Complexity of the search is only logarithmic in the required sample size:

[ O(\log n). ]

Two-proportion hypothesis test

Given simulated counts:

[ x_T,; n_T,; x_C,; n_C ]

calculate:

[ \hat p_T=\frac{x_T}{n_T}, \qquad \hat p_C=\frac{x_C}{n_C} ]

and pooled estimate:

[ \hat p = \frac{x_T+x_C}{n_T+n_C}. ]

Under the null (p_T=p_C):

[ Z = \frac{ \hat p_T-\hat p_C }{ \sqrt{ \hat p(1-\hat p) \left( \frac{1}{n_T}+\frac{1}{n_C} \right) } }. ]

Then for a two-sided test:

[ p=2\left[1-\Phi(|Z|)\right]. ]

Statsmodels provides proportions_ztest for an independent implementation against which your tests can be checked. [research citation in original report]

For small counts, provide Fisher's exact test as an alternative or warning state. SciPy's fisher_exact operates on contingency tables and returns an exact-test p-value; its documentation explicitly covers the 2×2 case. [research citation in original report]

Monte Carlo model

This is what differentiates Baseline Breaker from a normal power calculator.

For each simulated trial:

[ R_C \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(N_C,1-d_C) ]

[ R_T \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(N_T,1-d_T) ]

where (R) is the number retained.

Then:

[ X_C \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(R_C,p_C) ]

[ X_T \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(R_T,p_T). ]

Run the hypothesis test.

Define simulation success as:

[ I_r = \begin{cases} 1,&p_r<\alpha\text{ and effect is in intended direction}\ 0,&\text{otherwise} \end{cases} ]

and simulated power:

[ \widehat{\text{Power}}

\frac{1}{R} \sum_{r=1}^{R}I_r. ]

At (R=10{,}000), the worst-case Monte Carlo standard error is:

[ \sqrt{\frac{0.5(0.5)}{10{,}000}} =0.005. ]

So even at the maximum-variance case your simulation uncertainty is only about half a percentage point in standard-error terms.

Display that:

Estimated power     63.7%
Monte Carlo SE       0.5 pp
Replicates          10,000
Seed                    42

That is a small detail that will make the dashboard look scientifically thoughtful.

Vectorized simulator

Do not loop through 10,000 trials in Python.

Use NumPy arrays.

def simulate_binary_trials(params, reps=10_000, seed=42):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    retained_c = rng.binomial(
        params.n_control,
        1 - params.dropout_control,
        size=reps,
    )

    retained_t = rng.binomial(
        params.n_treatment,
        1 - params.dropout_treatment,
        size=reps,
    )

    events_c = rng.binomial(
        retained_c,
        params.p_control,
    )

    events_t = rng.binomial(
        retained_t,
        params.p_treatment,
    )

    rate_c = events_c / retained_c
    rate_t = events_t / retained_t

    pooled = (events_c + events_t) / (
        retained_c + retained_t
    )

    se = np.sqrt(
        pooled * (1 - pooled) *
        (1 / retained_c + 1 / retained_t)
    )

    z = (rate_t - rate_c) / se
    p_values = 2 * scipy.stats.norm.sf(np.abs(z))

    correct_direction = rate_t < rate_c

    success = (
        (p_values < params.alpha)
        & correct_direction
    )

    power = success.mean()

    mcse = np.sqrt(
        power * (1 - power) / reps
    )

    return SimulationResult(
        power=power,
        monte_carlo_se=mcse,
        ...
    )

NumPy's vectorized universal functions operate element-wise over arrays and support broadcasting, making them well suited to this style of batched numerical computation. [research citation in original report]

Fragility index

Fragility index is a good stretch feature, but it serves a different role.

Baseline sensitivity asks:

“How robust was the planning assumption?”

Fragility index asks:

“For a completed binary-outcome trial, how many outcome changes could flip a significance classification?”

Walsh and colleagues' original study examined the fragility of statistically significant dichotomous outcomes in randomized controlled trials; their reported median FI was 8 among the trials studied. [research citation in original report]

Basic algorithm:

def fragility_index(
    events_a,
    total_a,
    events_b,
    total_b,
    alpha=0.05,
):
    table = make_table(...)

    original_p = fisher_exact(table).pvalue

    if original_p >= alpha:
        return 0  # traditional FI not applicable as "significant → nonsignificant"

    # Identify the arm with fewer events according to
    # the chosen Walsh-style implementation.
    target_arm = choose_fragility_arm(table)

    flips = 0

    while fisher_exact(table).pvalue < alpha:
        if no_non_events_left(target_arm):
            return None

        convert_one_non_event_to_event(
            table,
            target_arm,
        )

        flips += 1

    return flips

Use scipy.stats.fisher_exact so the statistical test itself is not home-grown. [research citation in original report]

But add this warning directly beside it:

Fragility Index does not measure clinical importance, bias, or overall trial quality. It describes sensitivity of a significance threshold for a particular binary analysis.

Do not turn it into a giant red "TRIAL IS BAD" score.

Cluster-randomized designs

Also future-only.

For equal cluster size (m) and intracluster correlation (\rho), a classic first-order inflation factor is:

[ DE=1+(m-1)\rho. ]

Then approximately:

[ N_{\text{clustered}}

N_{\text{individual}} \times DE. ]

But do not pretend that formula handles arbitrary cluster trials.

Rutterford, Copas, and Eldridge note that the simplest sample-size method inflates an individually randomized calculation by a design effect, while more complicated designs require methods accounting for factors such as unequal cluster sizes, attrition, covariates, and repeated measures. [research citation in original report]

Risk and mitigation matrix

Risk Severity Why it matters Mitigation
Confusing disease prevalence with control event rate Critical Produces scientifically invalid assumptions Define baseline explicitly; never auto-use PLACES as trial risk
Protocol assumptions unavailable High NCT data alone may not reconstruct original power calculation Curate one trial manually; always permit manual input
Simulator disagrees with analytic power High Could indicate implementation bug or methodological difference Cross-check with statsmodels and explain method differences
Normal approximation fails for rare events High Z-test becomes questionable for sparse tables Warn on low expected counts; add Fisher exact option
Effect model changes silently as baseline changes High RR versus absolute difference gives different results Explicit effect-model toggle
Informative dropout Medium–high Independent dropout may underestimate sensitivity Label MCAR-like assumption; future outcome-dependent stress model
Monte Carlo randomness confuses users Medium Same inputs could slightly change estimates Fixed/reported seed and Monte Carlo SE
Simulation endpoint blocks API Medium CPU work can stall concurrent web requests Vectorize first; use worker process if needed
Heatmaps feel slow Medium Hurts live demo Coarse interactive grid + cached/refined grid
Fragility index overinterpreted Medium It is not a universal trial-quality score Present only as secondary completed-trial sensitivity metric
Hackathon scope explosion Critical Team spends weekend implementing five trial classes Freeze MVP to binary two-arm RCT
Product perceived as medical advice High Overstates tool capability Call it exploratory statistical stress testing, not protocol recommendation
Real trial example breaks on demo day High External API/network dependency Cache selected NCT metadata and always maintain synthetic fallback

FDA's E9(R1) guidance is particularly relevant to the risks of oversimplifying assumptions: it emphasizes clear formulation of objectives, treatment effects, design, analysis, and interpretation rather than treating a statistical number in isolation. [research citation in original report]

The final pitch

I would pitch Baseline Breaker like this:

Clinical trials are designed using assumptions about how often outcomes will occur, how large the treatment effect will be, and how many participants will drop out. If those assumptions are wrong, a trial that looked adequately powered on paper can become far less informative. Baseline Breaker is an interactive statistical stress-testing platform that simulates thousands of alternative trial realities and shows researchers exactly where a proposed design becomes vulnerable.

Then demo:

Assumed baseline: 20%
        ↓
Power: 80%

"What if the baseline is actually 10%?"

        ↓ slider

Power: 47%
        ↓
Heatmap changes
        ↓
Safety envelope changes

Then your closing line:

“Traditional power calculations tell you whether your trial works if your assumptions are right. Baseline Breaker shows you what happens when they're wrong.”

That is the version of this idea I would build for HackRU.
